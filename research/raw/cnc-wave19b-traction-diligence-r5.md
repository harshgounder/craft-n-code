# Seed Traction, Engineering Diligence, and Honest 24-Hour MVP Proof

## Data tables: traction and investor metrics first

The most important conclusion is that there is no defensible universal "seed traction number." SaaS benchmarks vary by ARR band, ACV, customer segment, business model, sample, and period. Several published datasets explicitly warn that private-company benchmarks are population-specific, and that public-company comparisons can mislead smaller private companies. [43] [30] The tables below therefore label the population and distinguish source-reported benchmarks from practical diligence questions.

## What seed investors actually underwrite

### The metric stack is a diagnostic system, not a checklist

ARR and MRR establish the recurring-revenue base, but neither proves durable demand on its own. ARR can be annualized from MRR, while MRR moves through new, expansion, contraction, churn, and reactivation; the investor wants the bridge rather than a headline number. [6] [36]

Retention is the compounding mechanism. GRR isolates whether the installed base survives without expansion, while NRR adds expansion and can exceed 100%; this is why NRR can look healthy while a product still loses many small accounts. [43] [30] For a seed company, show both logo and revenue retention because the two answer different questions: logos reveal breadth and customer concentration, while revenue reveals economic durability.

Acquisition efficiency is conditional on retention. A low CAC with poor GRR can be a leaky bucket, while a higher CAC with strong retention and expansion may become attractive if payback is credible. Bessemer explicitly ties payback targets to customer segment and says CAC payback is only after which the customer begins generating profit; applying an SMB target to enterprise sales is a category error. [43]

LTV:CAC is especially fragile at seed. Lifetime value is a forecast built from limited observations, so show the underlying churn, gross margin, cohort age, and whether expansion is observed or assumed. The useful investor question is not "is LTV:CAC 3x?" but "what observed cohort evidence makes the lifetime and margin assumptions credible?"

Gross margin is often misunderstood in AI and infrastructure-heavy products. A high subscription gross margin can coexist with lower total gross margin if delivery includes meaningful services or variable compute; KeyBanc's reported approximately seven-point spread between subscription and total gross margin illustrates why both should be disclosed.

### Red-flag matrix

| Signal | What the investor checks | Red flag | Honest response |
|---|---|---|---|
| MRR spike | Invoice-level reconciliation, recurring classification, customer start dates | One contract or unpaid pilot creates most of the growth | Show the bridge and label pilot, services, bookings, and cash separately. |
| High growth | Monthly cohort growth, source channel, retention, denominator | Growth is new-logo-only while old cohorts shrink | Show GRR, NRR, and churned ARR alongside growth. |
| High NRR | Cohort start/end MRR and expansion taxonomy | Small cohort, price increase, or one whale creates the result | Report customer-weighted and revenue-weighted views, plus expansion source. |
| Low churn | Logo and revenue churn definitions | Churn excludes downgrades, pauses, failed payments, or cancellations after period end | Define cancellation, contraction, reactivation, and denominator. |
| Attractive CAC | Fully loaded spend and attribution period | Founder labor, onboarding, and partner fees omitted | Present new-only and fully-loaded CAC with gross-margin adjustment. |
| Short payback | Gross-margin-adjusted contribution and time to cash | Uses projected expansion or annual prepayment without collection risk | Show observed payback by cohort and separate cash collection from revenue recognition. |
| 3x LTV:CAC | Cohort lifetime, margin, and churn evidence | Lifetime is modeled from too few months | Use a sensitivity range and state that LTV is provisional. |
| High gross margin | COGS policy and delivery costs | Compute, inference, support, or implementation costs treated as Opex | Show subscription and total gross margin separately. |
| Consumer engagement | Value event and cohort window | Downloads, notifications, or sessions without retained value | Define the activation event and show D1/D7/D30 or relevant product windows. |
| Deep-tech milestone | Test conditions, repeatability, relevant environment | A one-off lab demo is presented as production readiness | Name current TRL-like evidence and next de-risking experiment. |

The red flags above are practical diligence interpretations. They should not be presented as universal published thresholds. The source-backed benchmark ranges belong in the tables, while the decision rule is to demand definitions and cohort evidence before comparing a company with a benchmark.

## Traction presentation: make the evidence inspectable

### One primary metric on a monthly timeline

Choose one primary metric that corresponds to the product's value event. For B2B SaaS this may be ending MRR, new ARR, or qualified active accounts; for a consumer product it may be retained users performing the core action; for deep tech it may be a measured performance variable tied to the next commercialization milestone. Do not display all metrics with equal visual weight.

The monthly timeline should include actual dates, beginning and ending values, and the movements between them. For SaaS, the minimum bridge is beginning MRR plus new, expansion, reactivation, minus contraction and churn, equaling ending MRR; the source material explicitly emphasizes decomposing ARR into its components and separating new business from expansion. [36] For a consumer product, show acquisition cohort, activation event, return event, and monetization event rather than only a cumulative user total.

Use a consistent cutoff. If the latest month is partial, mark it partial and do not compare it with full months without qualification. If a customer has signed but not launched, place it in contracted or booked revenue only if that is the company's defined category; do not silently put it in live MRR.

### Cohort table and retention curve

A cohort table groups users or accounts by a shared start event, then follows the same group through consistent time intervals. Mixpanel describes cohort analysis as tracking a group over time, while SaaS Capital's NRR calculation explicitly uses customers present in both the beginning and ending comparison periods. [30] A cohort table prevents new users from masking old-user decay.

For SaaS, provide at least logo GRR, revenue GRR, NRR, and gross churn by monthly or quarterly cohort where sample size permits. For consumer, provide D1, D7, D30 or the product's natural usage interval, and disclose whether retention is classic, rolling, unbounded, or based on a specific return event. If the product is weekly by nature, D1/D7 may be less meaningful than week-1/week-4 retained core action.

A retention curve is more informative than a single percentage. A curve that falls sharply and then flattens suggests a retained core; a curve that continues falling suggests the product has not yet created durable habit or the acquisition channel is mis-targeted. This is interpretation, not a guaranteed diagnosis, so connect it to interviews and observed user behavior.

### Sean Ellis test and limits

The Sean Ellis survey asks active users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. The canonical response is "very disappointed," and the commonly cited threshold is 40%; the original survey framing describes it as a leading indicator found by benchmarking nearly a hundred startups.

The test is useful because it asks about perceived loss of value, but it is not a substitute for retention, payment, or repeated usage. Survivorship bias is a central limitation: users who are active enough to receive and answer the survey are not the same as all signups, churned users, or unactivated users. A founder should report respondent count, response rate, user segment, time since activation, channel, and the percentage of all invited users represented.

Do not use the 40% figure as a pass/fail gate for a pre-launch demo. A small sample of enthusiastic design partners can yield a high score while the product has no repeatable acquisition or willingness to pay. Pair the survey with a cohort curve, a behavioral value event, payment or commitment, and a clear statement of who was excluded.

### Recommended traction page structure

1. **Headline:** one primary metric and the exact period.
2. **Monthly chart:** actual values, not a smoothed trend line.
3. **Bridge:** new, expansion, contraction, churn, and reactivation for SaaS, or acquisition, activation, retained action, and monetization for consumer.
4. **Cohorts:** start month, cohort size, and retention definition.
5. **Unit economics:** CAC, payback, gross margin, and the denominator.
6. **Qualitative evidence:** customer quote or observed workflow, labeled as qualitative.
7. **Risks:** the largest unproven assumption and the next experiment.

The purpose is not to make the chart look inevitable. It is to make the investor able to reproduce the claim from the underlying records.

## Investor-facing technical diligence

Technical diligence asks whether the technology claims in the commercial story hold up and whether the company can deliver the next milestone without hidden liabilities. A contemporary checklist commonly covers architecture and scalability, code quality and delivery, team and key-person risk, security and compliance, IP and open-source licensing, data, and operational risk.

### Architecture and scale assumptions

The reviewer starts with the actual system diagram, not the aspirational one. The diagram should identify clients, services, databases, queues, third-party APIs, authentication, storage, deployment environment, observability, and data flows. It should explain where state lives, what is synchronous, what is asynchronous, and what happens when a dependency fails.

The most important seed-stage question is not "does this scale to one billion users?" It is "what assumptions would fail first if the next customer segment arrives?" State the expected traffic shape, tenant count, data volume, latency objective, concurrency, retention period, and cost driver. If those numbers are unknown, mark them **UNVERIFIED** and define the measurement needed.

A reviewer checks single points of failure, data migration strategy, backup and restore, rate limits, idempotency, retry behavior, tenancy isolation, and secrets handling. A monolith is not a red flag at seed; an undocumented architecture that the team cannot explain is. Conversely, a microservice diagram without operational evidence can indicate complexity rather than readiness.

### Tests and code quality

Test coverage is evidence about which code has been executed by tests, not a direct measure of test quality. Martin Fowler states that coverage helps find untested parts of a codebase but is of little use as a numeric statement of how good the tests are. [45] Investors should therefore inspect tests for critical paths, failure cases, authorization boundaries, data migrations, and external dependency behavior.

A seed audit asks whether tests run in CI, whether failures block merge, whether fixtures are deterministic, whether tests cover the value-producing workflow, and whether a new feature adds or updates tests. A high percentage with trivial assertions is weak evidence; a lower percentage with strong end-to-end coverage around payment, permissions, and data integrity may be more relevant.

Code churn should be interpreted as context, not a score. Rapid change can mean learning, but it can also mean instability, rework, or a founder rewriting the system without tests. Reviewers inspect commits and pull requests, identify abandoned migrations and repeated rewrites, and ask whether the current branch is deployable.

### Security and dependency risk

NIST's Secure Software Development Framework provides a structured set of practices for reducing software vulnerability risk; OpenSSF Scorecard is intended to generate a security signal for open-source projects; OWASP Dependency-Check is a software composition analysis tool that identifies known vulnerable components.

A seed repository should have a dependency manifest and lockfile where the ecosystem supports it, a process for security updates, a way to see transitive dependencies, and a documented exception process. GitHub Dependabot alerts are signals that GitHub detected vulnerable dependencies; they are not proof that all vulnerabilities are found or that every alert has been remediated.

Secret scanning can scan Git history for hardcoded credentials, including API keys, passwords, and tokens. A clean scan is useful evidence, but it does not prove secrets were never exposed elsewhere, that historical credentials were rotated, or that runtime access is least-privilege. A reviewer should ask for credential rotation history and cloud IAM configuration where the product handles sensitive data.

License diligence is not just a package list. The reviewer maps dependencies to licenses, checks attribution and notice obligations, identifies copyleft or source-disclosure conditions, and asks whether the product distributes a derivative work. Fenwick states that investors examine third-party technology licenses and pay special attention to copyleft terms that could require proprietary code disclosure. [57] OWASP and SPDX-style inventories improve repeatability, but legal interpretation remains fact-specific.

### IP ownership and chain of title

The company should be able to show signed agreements for founders, employees, contractors, agencies, and relevant consultants. Orrick states that securing assignments from software contractors is essential, and that the effectiveness of a transfer varies by jurisdiction. [56] Its U.S. discussion says contractors generally retain software IP unless rights are assigned in writing, and that a present assignment is needed rather than only a future promise. [56]

Fenwick's diligence guidance emphasizes clean chain of title, including pre-existing IP, work created during service, possible prior employers, university or government-funded research, bespoke joint development, and third-party technology licenses. [57] This is a common hidden seed risk because a fast prototype may have been built before incorporation or by a contractor whose agreement was informal.

The reviewer should request an IP schedule, assignment agreements, employment and contractor agreements, invention-assignment carve-outs, prior-employer conflicts, patent and trademark filings, open-source policy, and any joint-development agreement. If a document is missing, state the gap rather than claiming the company owns the code.

### Key-person and team risk

The reviewer tests whether the system is understandable and operable by more than one person. Evidence includes onboarding instructions, code ownership, deployment permissions, incident knowledge, runbooks, architecture decisions, and recent pull-request distribution. A repository where one founder authored every critical module is not automatically unacceptable at seed, but it creates execution and continuity risk.

A practical test is the "two-person handoff": can a second engineer clone the repository, configure a safe environment, run tests, deploy a non-production change, observe it, and roll it back using written instructions? If not, the company should label the system founder-dependent and make the next milestone knowledge transfer rather than cosmetic refactoring.

### What each repository signal can and cannot prove

A README can communicate project purpose, use, support, and maintainers, but it cannot prove that installation works. GitHub explicitly presents README content as project information and onboarding guidance, not as a correctness certificate. [51]

CI proves that a defined job passed in a defined environment. It does not prove that the job covers production behavior, that secrets are configured safely, that the test suite is meaningful, or that the deployment can be recovered. The reviewer should save the workflow definition, a passing run, a failing run, and the exact artifact or image produced.

Branch protection and CODEOWNERS prove that certain repository controls can be configured and enforced. They do not prove independent review if the same person approves, owns the repository, and can bypass protections. Inspect the effective settings, recent PRs, actor identities, and audit events.

Dependabot and secret scanning are valuable detection signals. Dependabot alerts exist when GitHub detects vulnerable dependencies; secret scanning checks Git history for hardcoded credentials. Neither substitutes for threat modeling, runtime configuration review, credential rotation, data-flow analysis, or legal license review.

Code history is evidence of behavior over time. It can reveal direct pushes, reversions, abandoned branches, repeated rewrites, or late security work, but it cannot reveal work done outside the repository. Ask for deployed commit hashes and compare them with the audited branch.

### A practical 2026 seed audit sequence

**Pass 1: identity and reproducibility.** Confirm repository ownership, organization membership, branch, deployed commit, license, README, setup commands, runtime versions, dependency lockfiles, and environment-variable contract. Clone from a fresh machine or disposable environment, run the documented setup, and record every undocumented intervention.

**Pass 2: value path.** Identify the one user workflow that creates the claimed value. Run it from a clean account, inspect authorization and data boundaries, test the failure path, and trace the resulting event or billing record. The investor is checking whether the product claim is connected to code and data, not whether every feature is complete.

**Pass 3: delivery.** Open recent PRs, inspect reviews and CI, check branch protection, inspect release tags and deployment workflow, identify the current production commit, and execute a safe rollback or dry-run rollback. Google SRE's canary guidance describes a pattern in which traffic can be moved between versions and rollback can be a reversal of routing, while noting the resource trade-off.

**Pass 4: risk.** Review dependency and license inventory, Dependabot and secret-scanning alerts, access controls, cloud accounts, backups, incident history, IP assignments, third-party data, and key-person concentration. Any missing artifact becomes an explicit risk and follow-up request rather than a silent assumption.

**Pass 5: next milestone.** Convert findings into a short list: must-fix before investment, acceptable seed risk, and evidence needed before the next round. A seed company does not need enterprise ceremony, but it does need to know which risks it is accepting.

## What a 24-hour team can honestly prove

### The honest line between demo and product

A 24-hour build can prove that a team can reduce a narrow problem to a working path, make tradeoffs, integrate a limited set of components, and produce a testable artifact. It can also generate evidence about user comprehension if real target users attempt the workflow. It cannot, by elapsed time alone, prove durable demand, retention, secure operations, production reliability, scalable economics, legal ownership, or a repeatable acquisition channel.

YC's MVP guidance emphasizes talking to users, launching quickly, getting feedback, and keeping the MVP lean. Paul Graham's advice is to do things that do not scale, because early founders can learn by manually helping users rather than prematurely building infrastructure. The implication is not "build a low-quality product"; it is "reduce the product to the smallest real learning loop."

### Table 5. 24-hour proof matrix

| Claim | Can a 24-hour build prove it? | Minimum honest evidence | What remains unproven |
|---|---|---|---|
| A user understands the problem | **Partially.** | Several target users can describe a recent instance and complete a narrow workflow without coaching. | Market size, willingness to pay, repeat demand. |
| A narrow workflow can work | **Yes, narrowly.** | A clean demo or test completes one path with explicit fixtures and known limitations. | Edge cases, reliability, integrations, scale. |
| Users value the output | **Partially.** | User performs the intended value action, asks to repeat it, or makes a concrete commitment; quote and behavior are recorded. | Retention and generalizability. |
| Product-market fit | **No.** | At most, a hypothesis and early qualitative signal; a Sean Ellis survey is not appropriate as a pass/fail test on a tiny demo cohort. | Cohort retention, repeated usage, acquisition, payment. |
| Revenue traction | **Only if real payment occurs.** | Paid transaction or signed customer commitment with clear terms, reconciled to a customer record. | Repeatability, gross margin, CAC, collection, churn. |
| Consumer retention | **No.** | A first activation cohort can be instrumented and a follow-up window started. | D1/D7/D30 or natural-cycle retention requires elapsed time. |
| SaaS NRR | **No.** | Instrument beginning MRR, expansion, contraction, and churn so future cohorts can be calculated. | NRR requires an observed beginning and ending cohort period. [30] |
| Deep-tech readiness | **Only for a narrow experiment.** | Measurement protocol, conditions, raw result, repeatability attempt, and explicit current readiness claim. | Relevant-environment performance, manufacturing, unit economics, adoption. [261-65] |
| Production security | **No.** | Threat model for the narrow path, no secrets in repository, basic dependency and permission review. | Complete security posture, penetration testing, incident response. |
| Scalability | **No, except a bounded load observation.** | State the test load, latency, cost, and bottleneck observed. | Future traffic shape, multi-tenant behavior, failure recovery. |
| Clean-machine reproducibility | **Yes for the narrow artifact.** | Fresh clone, documented setup, pinned dependencies, successful build/test/run. | Long-term reproducibility and production deployment. [306-08] |
| Rollback | **Partially.** | Versioned artifact, reversible code path, safe data plan, and a tested recovery command. | Recovery under real traffic and irreversible data migrations. |
| Repeatable sales | **No.** | Manual outreach log, conversations, objections, commitments, and next meetings. | Channel efficiency, CAC, sales cycle, forecastability. |

### A defensible 24-hour build plan

**Hour 0-2: choose the claim.** Write one falsifiable sentence: "For target user X, workflow Y produces outcome Z under condition Q." Define what counts as success and what would falsify the hypothesis. Select one primary value event and one observable failure event.

**Hour 2-4: recruit manually.** Identify a narrow list of real target users, not generic friends. Ask about a recent problem and current workaround, then invite them to try the narrow workflow. YC's first-customer guidance focuses on identifying the right buyers and starting conversations; the first ten customers rarely arrive from a generic automated funnel.

**Hour 4-14: build the smallest path.** Use a single tenant or controlled account if that is sufficient for the test. Manual back-office work is acceptable if disclosed. Avoid building billing, admin roles, generalized integrations, complex permissions, scaling infrastructure, and polished settings unless they are necessary to test the claim.

**Hour 14-17: instrument and harden the claim.** Add event logging for activation, value completion, error, and abandonment. Add at least one negative-path test and remove secrets from code. Record the exact environment, sample data, third-party dependencies, and known limitations.

**Hour 17-20: clean-machine verification.** A second person clones the repository and follows the README. Reproducible-build practice exists to make build results independently verifiable and to ensure source or environment changes explain generated differences. [306-07] If setup fails, record the failure; do not silently repair the environment and call the build reproducible.

**Hour 20-22: user test.** Watch target users attempt the workflow without rescuing them. Record time to first value, completion, confusion, workarounds, willingness to repeat, and willingness to pay or schedule a next session. A positive demo reaction is qualitative evidence, not retention.

**Hour 22-24: report honestly.** Ship the repository, demo URL if safe, test log, user evidence, metric definition, and a proof ledger with three columns: proved, suggested, and unverified. The investor-facing artifact should include the next experiment and the reason it resolves the largest remaining uncertainty.

### Manual recruitment is evidence, not a failure of scale

Early manual work is useful when it is deliberate and measurable. Maintain a prospect log containing target profile, source, date, problem evidence, current workaround, invitation, outcome, objection, commitment, and next action. Do not count a polite conversation as traction; count a completed workflow, payment, scheduled repeat, or explicit operational commitment according to the claim being tested.

The manual process should reveal whether the founder can identify the buyer, explain the problem in the buyer's language, deliver value, and learn from objections. It does not prove that the process will scale. The correct 24-hour claim is "we can recruit and serve the first users manually while learning," not "we have a repeatable acquisition engine."

## Evidence-first operating checklist

### Before the build

- Define one target user and one painful, recent problem.
- Define one value event and one disconfirming event.
- Decide whether the test is demand, usability, technical feasibility, or readiness; do not combine them into one vague success criterion.
- Pre-register the metric formula, window, denominator, and inclusion rules.
- Secure written IP assignments before treating contractor code as company-owned. [56]
- List third-party APIs, data, models, libraries, and license obligations.

### During the build

- Keep the scope narrow enough that a second person can explain it.
- Use real target users or clearly label proxy users.
- Record manual interventions instead of hiding them.
- Keep the repository history intact and link changes to issues or observed user evidence.
- Run tests in CI where possible, but do not use coverage percentage as a quality proxy. [45]
- Keep credentials in environment or secret storage, not source control; secret scanning is a detection layer, not a complete security program.

### After the build

- Re-run from a clean machine or disposable environment.
- Test the primary workflow and one failure path.
- Inspect the deployed commit and release artifact.
- Demonstrate a rollback or state precisely why rollback is not yet safe.
- Report user behavior, not only user praise.
- Separate paid, promised, invited, active, retained, and merely registered users.
- Write the next experiment around the largest unresolved risk.

## Comparative analysis and synthesis

The three company types require different evidence. SaaS traction is a recurring economic system: MRR, retention, expansion, gross margin, CAC, and payback are connected through cohorts. Consumer traction is a behavioral system: acquisition matters only if users activate, perform the value action, and return on a product-appropriate cadence. Deep tech is a staged de-risking system: performance evidence, relevant-environment validation, adoption readiness, IP, cost, and commercialization must move together.

The same metric can mean different things across contexts. A 100% NRR result from a small enterprise cohort is not comparable to a 100% NRR result from a large SMB base. A 30-day consumer retention number is meaningless without the product's natural usage cycle. A TRL label does not imply customer adoption, and a working demo does not imply production readiness. [30] [14]

The same tension appears in engineering. Fast code churn can be evidence of learning or evidence of uncontrolled rework. High test coverage can identify untested code while failing to demonstrate test quality. A clean Dependabot page can show no detected alert while leaving license, runtime, configuration, or undiscovered vulnerability risk. A protected branch can enforce checks while an administrator retains a silent bypass. [45]

The decision framework is therefore observation, mechanism, implication, recommendation. **Observation:** a metric or repository signal is present. **Mechanism:** explain how the signal could create or destroy value. **Implication:** state the investor risk if the mechanism is wrong. **Recommendation:** request the smallest additional evidence that resolves the uncertainty. This avoids both vanity metrics and diligence theater.

For a 24-hour team, the investable proof is not the quantity of code. It is the quality of the evidence loop: a narrow user problem, a working value path, observed target-user behavior, a reproducible artifact, explicit limits, and a next experiment. The company should be rewarded for learning velocity only when the learning is falsifiable and the evidence is inspectable.

## Practical decision rules

1. **Do not benchmark without population context.** Require source, year, ARR or ARPA band, customer segment, sample, and percentile.
2. **Do not accept a headline metric without a bridge.** MRR must reconcile; NRR must use the same cohort; CAC must name spend and denominator.
3. **Do not call a survey PMF.** Use Sean Ellis as a supplemental leading indicator with respondent and selection disclosures.
4. **Do not call a prototype production.** Name the user, environment, load, data, security, and rollback limits.
5. **Do not call a repository secure because scans are green.** Review history, runtime controls, permissions, dependencies, and incidents.
6. **Do not call code company-owned because it was paid for.** Confirm written present assignments and jurisdiction-specific terms. [44] [56]
7. **Do not use coverage as a score.** Inspect critical-path assertions and failure behavior. [45]
8. **Do not automate recruitment before learning the buyer.** Manual outreach is acceptable; unmeasured outreach is not.
9. **Do not hide missing data.** Use **UNVERIFIED**, define the next measurement, and show the risk.
10. **Do not confuse speed with scale.** A one-day build can prove a narrow experiment and team execution under constraint, not durable demand or operational maturity.
