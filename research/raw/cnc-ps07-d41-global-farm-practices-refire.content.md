# Global Flood and Cyclone Farm Wisdom for Odisha

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **No single practice is the answer**: The strongest global pattern is a layered portfolio - forecast and warning, crop timing and genetics, raised or floating production, protected seed, livestock evacuation, drainage, and recovery support. The early-warning literature organizes this as four linked pillars: risk knowledge, monitoring and forecasting, warning communication, and preparedness to respond [30].
- **Predictable floods can become a production input**: In Vietnam, floating rice rises with seasonal water; farmers release fish after inundation and harvest both rice and fish after the flood. The trade-off is substantial: floating-rice yield is only one-half to one-third that of high-yielding varieties [31].
- **Flood type matters more than the practice's country of origin**: Cambodia's recession rice relies on natural flooding, while deepwater rice is used at depths above about 0.8 m and up to 4 m [1][8]. These fit slow, seasonal freshwater floods, not fast cyclone surge, saline water, or embankment breaches.
- **Seed protection is the most immediately transferable vernacular measure**: Flood-prone farmers can raise storage platforms, plaster walls with water-impermeable material, or use waterproof community stores [17]. This should become an automatic pre-flood advisory for every seed-holding household.
- **Water engineering spans plot to watershed scales**: Bangladesh baira beds float above prolonged waterlogging; China's dyke-pond system links drainage with fish, mulberry, and silk; Sri Lankan cascades connect tanks within micro-catchments; the Netherlands increases river capacity by moving dykes, lowering floodplains, and adding flood channels [39][36][12][32]. Odisha can copy plot-level principles more readily than capital-intensive landscapes.
- **Community institutions are part of the technology**: Bangladesh's Cyclone Preparedness Programme relays official meteorological warnings and supports sheltering, rescue, medical attention, and recovery [18]. A farm advisory engine therefore needs trusted village relays and human escalation, not SMS alone.
- **Redundant, accessible communication is evidence-based**: No single channel reaches everyone; people respond better to warnings from trusted sources through redundant, context-appropriate channels [30]. Odisha's Ama Krushi model already provides an IVR menu, live-agent access, tailored advisories, and retrievable message history [25].
- **Crop resistance complements, but does not replace, evacuation and storage**: Swarna-Sub1 can withstand complete submergence for roughly two to three weeks [20]. It is relevant to freshwater submergence, but it does not solve saline intrusion, high wind, livestock loss, or contaminated seed.
- **Recovery systems matter as much as pre-disaster advice**: The United States' Emergency Watershed Protection approach supports debris removal, bank reshaping, drainage repair, levee repair, and reseeding [29]. The transferable principle is a geotagged damage-to-assistance workflow, not the US financing model itself.
- **Several attractive prompt claims are filler or category errors**: Nigerian evidence identifies water hyacinth as damaging fish production, water quality, and infrastructure, not as a verified Niger Delta floating-farm material [26]. Thailand's floating markets are commerce, Arkansas flood irrigation is controlled irrigation, and Sudan's Gezira is a large irrigation scheme; none should be labeled a cyclone or natural-flood adaptation without additional evidence.

**Grade key:** **A** = field-ready Odisha analogue after normal local validation; **B** = promising pilot or adapted analogue; **C** = indirect principle, different agroecology, or major infrastructure/institutional burden; **D** = unsupported, contradicted, misclassified, or unsuitable for direct transfer.

## 4. WHAT IS MISSING

1. **Operational thresholds.** Most heritage sources explain mechanisms, not machine-ready values such as safe water velocity, bed height, drainage capacity, wind limit for tree work, livestock loading time, salinity threshold, or seed moisture. These must come from Odisha research stations, district plans, veterinary services, and field trials. The Odisha Crop Contingency Plan 2025 contains dedicated flood and pre/post-cyclone sections and lists stress-tolerant and short-duration paddy availability, but much of the extracted PDF's detailed text is not machine-readable [28].

2. **Cyclone wind and storm-surge evidence.** The best-documented global practices largely address slow freshwater inundation. Floating rice, recession sowing, chinampas, waru waru, and tank cascades do not establish safety under high wind, wave action, saline surge, lightning, or rapidly breached embankments.

3. **Species-specific livestock procedures.** The FAO source establishes the need for planned evacuation [27], but the Odisha engine still needs local shelter capacity, cattle/buffalo/goat/poultry handling times, fodder and water quantities, vaccination and disease protocols, ear-tag or owner identification, carcass disposal, and routes that remain passable at forecast water levels. Floodwater can contain sewage, manure, and chemicals, so access to drinking water must be separately assessed [43].

4. **Economics and labor.** Vietnamese floating rice can reduce purchased inputs and produce fish, but its rice yield is one-half to one-third that of high-yielding varieties [31]. Waru waru faces abandonment and livelihood pressures [37]. A technically effective practice can fail when labor, tenure, market access, or opportunity cost is ignored.

5. **Equity and last-mile failure.** Early warnings often fail for geographically isolated, marginalized, or economically vulnerable communities [30]. Farm profiles therefore need preferred language, phone sharing, disability, migration, livestock ownership, tenancy, and access to transport or shelters. These are operational risk variables, not optional demographics.

6. **Independent verification for requested names.** The evidence base remains insufficient for Thai durian/mangosteen raised flood mounds, Philippine coconut bracing and formal farmer typhoon calendars, Japanese "bofurin," tidal rice, Japanese generic home-garden resilience, Han flood-recession agriculture, Indonesian raft farming, sago as a flood-response protocol, Pakistan khaddar recession melons, Bangladesh machan, and Madagascar filovana.

**Decision-ready insight:** Treat these gaps as a research backlog, not empty fields for generative AI. Every automated rule needs a named source, hazard scope, valid location, contraindications, and an owner responsible for local approval.

## 5. HOW IT FEEDS THE ADVISORY ENGINE

### 5.1 Architecture: four pillars plus a farm digital profile

The engine should implement the four people-centered early-warning pillars: **risk knowledge -> monitoring and forecasting -> warning dissemination -> preparedness to act** [30]. IMD supplies the authoritative hazard signal; the platform should not generate a competing cyclone warning. It adds farm consequences by joining that signal to:

- geolocation, elevation, embankment side, drainage outlet, waterlogging history, and salinity;
- crop, variety, sowing date, growth stage, harvest readiness, seed stock, and storage elevation;
- livestock species/count, evacuation route, shelter capacity, fodder, drinking water, and owner identification;
- pond/aquaculture status, likely overflow direction, and power/pump availability;
- household language, IVR preference, phone sharing, mobility constraints, and trusted village contact.

Ama Krushi demonstrates an appropriate delivery pattern: a toll-free shortcode, IVR, live agent, tailored weekly advice, and access to message history [25]. The global evidence adds a crucial constraint: no single communication channel reaches everyone [30]. Each critical alert should therefore use SMS plus Odia IVR, village volunteer confirmation, and escalation to a live agent.

### 5.2 Practice-to-rule conversion

| Rule ID and source practice | Machine trigger | Advisory action | Guardrail and grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R1 Official-alert lock** - CPP | Valid IMD district/geofence alert received | Repeat hazard type, location, issue time, validity, and official action; notify village relay and extension desk | Never paraphrase a watch as a warning. CPP demonstrates official-signal relay [18]. **A** |
| **R2 Redundant last mile** - people-centered EWS and Ama Krushi | Severe alert plus unacknowledged farmer message | Send concise SMS; place Odia IVR call; retry; notify trusted village contact; offer live agent | No single channel reaches everyone [30]. Record acknowledgment, not merely delivery. **A** |
| **R3 Flood-safe seed** - Practical Action | Farm stores seed/grain and forecast water may reach storage | Dry and label seed; seal an inner container; move above predicted water; duplicate a lot at a second safe site or community store | Raised and waterproof storage is documented [17]. Do not claim jute sacks or earthen pots alone are floodproof [17]. **A** |
| **R4 Livestock movement plan** - FAO | Forecast route-cutoff time is approaching and safe shelter is confirmed | Identify animals and owners; move early by species order; carry fodder, medicines, and clean-water plan; confirm head count at destination | No universal fixed lead time. Use route, species, daylight, handlers, and shelter capacity. **A** |
| **R5 Livestock water safety** - post-flood guidance | Floodwater contacted wells, troughs, manure, chemicals, or carcasses | Block unsafe water; use tested or approved clean supply; monitor animals and request veterinary help | Floodwater contamination can infect animals [43]. **A** |
| **R6 Submergence-tolerant paddy planning** - Swarna-Sub1 | Preseason or replanting decision; freshwater submergence risk; approved local seed available | Offer Swarna-Sub1 or locally approved equivalent, with duration, seed availability, and district recommendation | Swarna-Sub1 tolerates roughly two to three weeks of submergence [20]. Not a salinity or wind solution. **A** |
| **R7 Lodging-risk varietal score** - Japan | Preseason variety ranking for cyclone-prone block | Rank approved varieties by lodging resistance, maturity, yield, and local trial performance | Research confirms typhoon-related lodging traits [7], not automatic suitability of a Japanese variety. **B** |
| **R8 Seasonal floating-rice/rice-fish pilot** - Vietnam | Predictable slow freshwater rise, appropriate land, long lead time, approved variety, and fish escape controls | Offer only as an extension-supervised seasonal pilot | Lower rice yield and hydrologic dependence must be disclosed [31]. Never activate for cyclone surge. **B** |
| **R9 Floating vegetable bed** - Bangladesh baira | Prolonged freshwater waterlogging, safe biomass, sheltered water, household vegetable need | Provide a locally tested bed recipe, anchoring, crop list, and sanitation warning | Baira is verified for vegetables and spices [39]. Do not recommend invasive or contaminated hyacinth; Nigerian evidence shows harm in aquaculture [26]. **B** |
| **R10 Raised bed plus perimeter drainage** - chinampa/waru waru | Recurrent shallow waterlogging; suitable soil and outlet; non-paddy vegetable or nursery plot | Raise the root zone, route water through maintained channels, stabilize edges, and inspect after heavy rain | Inspiration is sound [37][33], but dimensions require Odisha trials. **B** |
| **R11 Flood-recession sowing gate** - Cambodia/Mali | Water level is falling; field accessible; soil wet but workable; salinity and contamination acceptable; crop can mature before next hazard | Recommend approved short-duration crop and sowing window | Recession farming follows water withdrawal [1][13]. Never advise solely because satellite water area shrank. **B** |
| **R12 Drain-pond integration** - China/Australia | Existing freshwater pond and field drainage can be safely connected | Capture manageable drainage, prevent fish escape, reuse only after water-quality and overflow checks | Dyke-pond integration and tail-water reuse are documented [36][35]. Agrichemical and disease risks require local limits. **B** |
| **R13 Community tank/ditch maintenance** - Sri Lanka/subak | Pre-monsoon inspection or blocked community drainage detected | Assign desilting, gate, bund, culvert, and outlet tasks to named water-user groups; log completion | Governance is the transferable mechanism [12][2]. Major works need engineers and permits. **B/C** |
| **R14 Controlled overflow corridor** - Room for the River | District hydrologic plan identifies safe storage or conveyance land | Protect drainage corridors and designated overflow zones from obstruction; trigger authority action, not individual farmer action | The Dutch model increases river capacity [32]. Requires compensation, land-use authority, and hydraulic modeling. **C** |
| **R15 Post-flood damage-to-assistance** - USDA | Alert expires and authorities declare access safe | Photograph and geotag crop, seed, livestock, pond, bund, fence, and drainage damage; triage contamination; clear debris safely; restore drainage; route claims and input support | Mirrors the recovery sequence of debris, banks, drainage, structures, and reseeding [29]. **A** |
| **R16 Evidence quarantine** - noise log | Retrieved practice lacks source, mechanism, hazard fit, or local approval | Show "research lead - no farmer advisory" to administrators; send nothing to farmers | Prevents unsupported labels from becoming dangerous instructions. **A governance rule** |

### 5.3 Message design for low literacy

Each farmer-facing message should contain only: **hazard -> farm consequence -> three prioritized actions -> deadline -> confirmation/help option**. Use short Odia sentences and spoken IVR; repeat numbers; avoid technical terms such as "return period" or "lodging index." Critical instructions should support keypad acknowledgment and a live-agent branch. Community relays should receive a different operational message listing unconfirmed households, shelter capacity, livestock-route status, and blocked drains.

Advice should be stage-aware. "Harvest now" is unsafe if wind has arrived, grain is immature, evacuation time is short, or storage is unprotected. The rule engine must first reserve time for human safety, then livestock movement, then mature crop salvage and asset protection. Post-event messages should wait for official access clearance and distinguish freshwater inundation, saline surge, contaminated water, wind damage, and prolonged waterlogging.

**Decision-ready insight:** The product is not a chatbot that recites global practices. It is a constrained event-condition-action system whose knowledge objects carry source, hazard type, lead time, crop stage, contraindications, grade, and local approver.

| Madagascar filovana | **UNVERIFIED** | Targeted searches did not establish the named practice. | Reject. |

The main noise pattern is **geographic leakage**: a real practice from one place is copied into another place without evidence. The second is **hazard leakage**: irrigation, drought storage, or heritage terraces are relabeled as cyclone resilience. The third is **mechanism omission**: a colorful name is supplied without dimensions, timing, users, or failure conditions.

## 7. VERDICT

### Synthesis: five strategies, five different jobs

| Strategy | Representative cases | Mechanism | Best time horizon | Main trade-off | Odisha decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Resist water or wind** | Swarna-Sub1; Japanese lodging research; dykes | Genetic tolerance or structural exclusion | Preseason to long-term | Can fail outside design conditions; does not cover all hazards | Deploy locally approved genetics now; engineer structures separately. |
| **Accommodate water** | Vietnam floating rice; Bangladesh baira; Cambodian deepwater rice | Crop or bed remains productive as water rises | Seasonal | Lower yield, specialized hydrology, anchoring, water quality | Pilot only in mapped freshwater waterlogging zones. |
| **Exploit recession** | Cambodia rice; Mali sorghum; historic Nile basins | Plant into residual moisture after controlled or natural retreat | Days to seasonal | Timing, contamination, salinity, land access | Build a sensor-gated sowing module after local trials. |
| **Store and redirect water** | Chinese dyke-pond; Sri Lankan tanks; NSW storage; Dutch flood corridors | Drainage, detention, reuse, or designated overflow | Seasonal to multi-decade | Land, capital, maintenance, downstream impacts | Use farm drains and existing ponds first; escalate watershed works to authorities. |
| **Organize collective action** | CPP, subak, Ama Krushi, USDA recovery | Trusted relays, shared maintenance, response, and assistance workflow | Hours to permanent | Institutional capacity and exclusion risk | Make this the platform's backbone, not an add-on. |

The non-obvious conclusion is that the most technologically impressive systems are not the most transferable. Room for the River, subak, tank cascades, and Ifugao terraces are powerful because institutions maintain them over whole landscapes. A phone cannot reproduce that infrastructure. It can, however, trigger inspections, assign maintenance, document completion, escalate blockages, and ensure that community action occurs before the hazard.

A second tension is between production and protection. Vietnamese floating rice accepts lower rice yield in exchange for fish, sediment, lower external inputs, and flood compatibility [31]. High-yield systems may maximize normal-year output but create exposure when they suppress or ignore seasonal flooding. The advisory engine should present this as a farm-specific trade-off, not claim that one system is universally superior.

A third tension is ecological. Bangladesh uses water hyacinth and other residues in a documented floating-bed system [39], while Nigerian evidence identifies water hyacinth as an aquaculture and infrastructure threat [26]. The correct lesson is not "use water hyacinth everywhere." It is "validate material, ecology, contamination, and disposal in the target place."

### Final decision

Build the Odisha platform in three evidence gates:

1. **Deploy Grade A operations first:** official-alert relay, redundant SMS/IVR and village confirmation, elevated waterproof seed storage, approved submergence-tolerant and short-duration seed menus, species-specific livestock evacuation, drinking-water safety, drainage inspection, and geotagged post-flood recovery. These measures are low-regret and align with Odisha's existing flood/cyclone planning categories [28].

2. **Pilot Grade B adaptations with research partners:** baira-style vegetable beds, raised nursery plots, rice-fish in predictable freshwater zones, recession sowing, drain-pond reuse, lodging-based variety ranking, and water-user maintenance groups. Every pilot should record flood depth/duration, salinity, labor, cost, yield, failures, and farmer acceptance before the rule reaches production.

3. **Quarantine Grade C/D material:** show it in an administrator research library, not in farmer messages. Large infrastructure requires authority and engineering; unsupported names require primary documentation; contradicted practices remain blocked.

**Verdict:** The global wisdom layer is feasible, but only as a source-traceable, hazard-specific rule registry. The winning system will not be the one that knows the most colorful traditions. It will be the one that reliably distinguishes seasonal freshwater flooding from cyclone wind and saline surge, sends three safe actions through channels farmers trust, and refuses to invent instructions when evidence is missing.
