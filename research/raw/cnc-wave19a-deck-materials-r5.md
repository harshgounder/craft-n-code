## Executive findings and status

This run has answered the substantive research questions. The strongest primary standard is YC's combination of two complementary documents: a ten-slide seed template and a seven-question pitching framework. The template gives the deck order, while the seven questions explain the reasoning and language behind the order. YC's recurring standard is not visual theatrics; it is clarity, concision, concrete language, meaningful numbers, a coherent narrative, and a direct ask. [6] [17]

The evidence base is broad enough for a deep report and exceeds 80 unique discovered URLs. It is not equally strong across all URLs. YC and DocSend pages are first-party guidance or first-party analytics pages; TechCrunch is an independent teardown source; Airbnb, Coinbase, Notion, Stripe, Buffer, and Dropbox pages are mostly secondary analyses or hosted artifacts. The report therefore separates documented deck facts from reviewer interpretation and marks Linear and Plaid as (UNVERIFIED) rather than filling the gap with internet folklore. [13] [39]

The key practical conclusion is that a seed deck is a compressed decision document, not a business-plan encyclopedia. YC says that founders should strive for clarity and concision, warns against a market treatise, and says the seed deck is intentionally simple in design. The investor should be able to answer five questions quickly: what is this, who needs it, why now or why this team, what evidence exists, and what does the financing purchase? [6] [6]

## 1. YC's complete seed deck standard

### The YC slide sequence

YC's seed template has ten practical units. The first is the title page, containing the company name and one-line description. The second is the problem, where the founder clearly states the problem and gives particulars about how it affects real people or businesses. The third is the solution, which should explain what the company does in as few words as possible and describe concrete benefits. [6] [6]

The fourth and fifth units are progress and metrics. YC says to show traction if it exists and to make numbers clear and meaningful. A chart does not need an impossibly smooth curve, and context should sit next to the chart when it makes an important statistic interpretable. YC also says to add more metrics when they are useful and notes that revenue is preferable where available, though another meaningful metric can still be acceptable. [6]

The sixth unit is insight or differentiation. The founder must tell the investor what makes the company special, what makes the approach work, and what the founders know that others do not. This is where the deck becomes more than a description of a product. It explains the mechanism of advantage. [6] [17]

The seventh unit is business model. YC acknowledges that seed companies may not know every detail, but says they should know a lot of them and lay the model out. If the model is complicated, more slides are allowed, but the default remains concise. A professional deck says how users become revenue; an amateur deck lists several possible monetization ideas and asks the investor to choose one. [6] [17]

The eighth unit is market. The founder must explain whether the market is big, whether the company can make it big, and how much money the company can make. YC's wording is deliberately investor-centered: the deck should convince the investor that the investment can produce substantial returns, not merely that the product is interesting. [6]

The ninth unit is team. YC calls team especially important at seed and says the slide should explain why the founders are particularly suited to the problem. It should be about founders, not a parade of advisors. The companion pitch guide adds the number of founders, technical-founder status, how long the founders have known one another, full-time status, equity split, and unusually relevant credentials. [6] [17]

The tenth unit is ask and use of funds. State how much money is needed and what the capital gets the company. A credible one-year destination, especially one that could make the company Series A ready, is powerful because it turns a financing amount into an operating plan. [6]

### YC's seven questions behind the slides

YC's pitching guide condenses the process into seven questions: what do you do, how big is the market, what is your progress, what is your unique insight, what is your business model, who is on your team, and what do you want? YC says that answering all seven succinctly puts founders well ahead of the curve. [17]

The first question is the linchpin. YC recommends the simplest language possible and says the description should be “predigested.” If the product is difficult to explain abstractly, walk through the user's action and result. The Google example is a website with a box where users type questions and receive websites that answer them, rather than a grand but less immediately useful statement about organizing the world's information. [17]

The market question supports either a pre-existing market or a new category. For a pre-existing market, research it. For a new product, estimate the number of customers who would want it and what they could be charged. YC explains top-down and bottom-up methods, but prefers bottom-up because it forces the founder to define the customer rather than claim that an enormous demographic is the market. [17]

Progress is not simply a list of accomplishments. YC defines the relevant idea as the ratio between what has been done and how long the company has worked on it. Product development and customers come first; fundraising and business-development deals are distant second-order evidence. This matters for the deck: show the velocity of execution, not only the existence of activity. [17]

Unique insight is a higher bar than saying that a problem exists. It is an explanation of what the founder understands about the problem that others do not. YC's Gmail example is that the inbox is a personal database of communications and documents, so users would not want to delete messages; the product implication was enough storage that deletion was unnecessary. “People need better email” is not a unique insight. [17]

YC is unusually hostile to a potpourri of business models. It says companies that have not figured out monetization may grow large and then turn on advertising or copy the dominant model, while a small subset proposes a new model that makes sense because the product changes the market. The deck should own a simple model rather than display virtual goods, product placement, ads, contests, and other options as a list. [17]

The team question asks for specific founder evidence, not prestige decoration. Technical coverage, working status, founder relationship, equity split, and relevant domain credentials are useful. A high GPA or a generic “worked at Google” line is not necessarily useful; a credential directly related to a difficult regulated or technical problem can be. [17]

Finally, YC says not to dance around the ask. If the founder wants investment, ask for investment. “What do you think?” and “Do I have a good idea?” are bad questions because they do not tell the investor how to help. The deck should end with a concrete request and a concrete next step. [17]

### YC mistakes and editing rules

YC's central mistake is muddled communication. The founder tries to make a seed company sound more sophisticated by adding market philosophy, jargon, acronyms, marketing language, or abstract terms such as “platform.” YC's editing advice is intentionally blunt: make the explanation sound dumber than the founder thinks it should. [17]

The Email Test operationalizes that advice. Write a two-sentence explanation, send it to a smart friend, and ask the friend to explain it back in different words. If the friend asks clarifying questions or cannot restate the idea, the pitch needs revision. Email is useful because the founder cannot rely on live conversation to patch an unclear sentence. [17]

The deck version of the same mistake is overbuilding. YC says the template is a model for a seed deck, not a treatise on a market or a worldview. It says the simple truth is that most seed companies do not yet have enough meaningful detail to justify a sprawling narrative, and pretending otherwise makes investors get lost. [6]

YC does not prohibit additional slides. It says the one-slide limit applies only to one location in the example deck, while other slides should be treated as the first slide of a set. Keeping a set at n=1 is ideal, more can be acceptable, and n>3 is probably too many. This is better interpreted as a compression rule than as a rigid total-slide rule. [6]

## 2. Case studies: what real decks reveal

### Airbnb: problem compression and category creation

The Airbnb case is widely circulated as an 11-slide 2008 deck and also as an original 2009 deck. The source disagreement is material: one analysis labels it 2008, another calls the original 2009 deck, and the reported first-round amount is not perfectly consistent across secondary pages. The correct practice is to cite the exact version being discussed and avoid pretending that every online reconstruction is one canonical file. [9] [18]

What survives across the versions is a remarkably compressed narrative. Airbnb starts with a direct proposition about booking rooms with locals, then establishes the problem, explains the marketplace, gives market and competition context, shows the product or customer experience, and closes with team and financing logic. The deck works because the reader can understand the transaction without first learning a new vocabulary. [9] [18]

The professional lesson is not “use the Airbnb slide titles.” It is to make a category-creating product legible through an existing human behavior. The deck does not ask the investor to begin with a grand market abstraction; it makes the proposed exchange concrete. That mechanism lets the problem, solution, market, and business model reinforce one another.

The criticism is equally useful. The historical deck benefited from a much earlier market context and cannot be copied mechanically. Later redesign analyses identify places where modern founders might add clarity, hierarchy, or evidence, while the original artifact itself should remain distinct from retrospective design commentary. Treat the deck as a case study in compression, not as proof that every sparse slide is good. [18]

### Coinbase: educate first, then convert education into a market thesis

The Coinbase seed deck is commonly described as an 11-slide bare-bones deck that raised $600K. A separate teardown calls it a 2012 deck and praises its simplicity, traction metrics, and powerful positioning while noting missing slides. The first-person source from Brian Armstrong says the attached deck was essentially the slides used for YC Demo Day, which supports its historical relevance but does not by itself expose every slide's content in the available text. [4] [27] [28]

The deck's mechanism is education. Rather than assume investors already understand Bitcoin, the opening section explains the value proposition and then connects that education to Coinbase's product and market. This is a good model for a technical or unfamiliar category: the deck spends its scarce attention budget teaching one idea that changes how the rest of the company is evaluated. [46]

The deck also illustrates selective evidence. DocSend's own examples page says clarity is a founder's best friend and describes Coinbase as selecting traction slides that portrayed the business positively. The lesson is not to cherry-pick misleading metrics; it is to choose the few metrics that answer the investment question and explain them clearly. [12]

The criticism is that simplicity can become incompleteness. The teardown explicitly mentions missing slides. A seed company should not add a slide merely to satisfy a checklist, but it should answer the investor's unavoidable questions: customer, market, model, team, and what the money buys. Coinbase's compactness worked because the central story was unusually memorable; most founders need to preserve that economy while adding missing decision evidence. [27]

### Stripe: useful structure, weak provenance discipline

A secondary deck library documents an eight-slide Stripe artifact, labels it a 2010 original seed pitch deck, and associates it with a claimed $2M raise. Another Slideshare item is described only as a Stripe pitch deck designed by Zlides and has no verifiable date, slide count, or seed status in the available evidence. Therefore, the report can analyze the documented eight-slide reconstruction but must not present every circulating Stripe deck as an authenticated original. [22] [15]

The reconstruction is valuable because it shows how a developer product can be framed around a painful implementation problem rather than a feature list. The strongest professional signal is a crisp explanation of who the user is and why the product removes a high-friction step. The deck's value is in its narrative architecture, not in the later company's scale or the retrospective headline that it raised billions of dollars. [25]

The main reviewer criticism is epistemic rather than visual: provenance. A polished slide reconstruction can be mistaken for a primary artifact. This is a caution for founders and researchers alike. A deck teardown should disclose whether it uses original slides, a hosted PDF, a founder recollection, or a modern analyst's reconstruction.

### DeckMatch: the teardown is the evidence

TechCrunch describes DeckMatch as a tool that evaluates pitch decks and reviewed the deck it used to raise a $1M seed round. That makes the case unusually relevant to this report: the company is presenting a deck about the same investor workflow that the audience is performing. [13] [13]

The case demonstrates a useful self-referential test. When the product is abstract, show the workflow and decision consequence. The deck's most useful professional signal is that it makes a specialized problem concrete enough for a non-specialist reader to follow. The reviewer discussion should be separated into what the slides claim and what TechCrunch thinks the slides accomplish.

The caution is not to overstate what snippets prove. The TechCrunch page is the source for slide-level claims; a headline confirming a $1M seed round is not enough to reconstruct the entire deck. In a final working deck, each slide should be traceable to a source or company fact, not filled with generic language because the researcher knows the company exists. [13]

### Notion: memorable thesis, secondary evidence

The Notion reconstruction opens with “The Future of Work” and is commonly described as an early seed deck associated with a $2M raise. The available full-page analysis documents the slide list and content, but it remains a secondary analysis rather than a source with an explicit original-file provenance statement. [5]

The professional signal is thesis-led framing. A memorable category statement can help the investor understand why the product is not just another feature. The risk is that a broad phrase such as “future of work” becomes empty unless the next slides show the precise user, product behavior, and economic mechanism.

Notion is therefore a useful contrast with Coinbase. Coinbase spends early attention teaching an unfamiliar market; Notion uses a category thesis to frame a familiar productivity problem. Both approaches work only if the next slide makes the abstract opening concrete.

### Uber: direct product idea, visible historical mistakes

TechCrunch describes the Uber artifact as a $200K pre-seed deck from 2008 and says it evaluates what Uber got right and where it made “spectacularly silly” mistakes. The source's framing is important because it prevents survivor bias: a successful company can have a deck with weak or embarrassing elements. [24]

The deck's directness is its strength. A mobile-enabled on-demand car service is easy to visualize, and the presentation connects user need, dispatch, and service delivery. The deck does not need a long explanation of a hidden technical architecture before the reader understands the customer transaction. [47]

Its mistakes are equally instructive. A founder should not copy a historical deck's weak claims, awkward terminology, or overconfident assumptions just because the company later succeeded. The correct professional standard is to retain the clear product flow while replacing unsupported market or business-model assertions with current, labeled evidence.

### Buffer: traction can rescue a weak problem slide, but should not be used as permission to omit the problem

Buffer's 2011 deck is described by one teardown as having raised $450K, with real traction and clear unit economics compensating for a weak problem slide. Public Slideshare listings describe a deck used to raise roughly half a million dollars and emphasize user growth and revenue. The figures differ, so the report preserves the source-specific amounts rather than collapsing them into one “official” number. [26] [42]

The case exposes a tension in seed deck standards. YC says the problem must be clear, but strong evidence can cause an investor to tolerate an imperfect narrative. That is a historical outcome, not a recommendation. Founders without Buffer-level proof should not assume that a weak problem slide will be rescued by a few vanity metrics.

The professional lesson is to connect traction to the problem. A revenue line matters because it demonstrates that a defined customer experiences enough value to pay. A user count matters when the deck explains who the users are, how they behave, and why the growth is meaningful.

### Encore: clear value proposition, but proof must follow the claim

Encore's deck contains 24 slides and opens with a cover, a team slide stating that the founders spent eight years scaling Spotify Premium, and two problem slides about modern software and slow software development. TechCrunch says the evolving positioning slides carve out space in a competitive market and that the message encouraging developers to focus on product rather than infrastructure is compelling. [2]

The deck's value proposition is described as extremely clear. Slides 11 and 12 explain that features and fixes suffer avoidable delays, while later slides visualize the same idea. The reviewer considers repetition worthwhile when it makes the core message easier to visualize. [2]

The criticism is a model for professional review. The deck claims a reduction from hours to minutes; the reviewer asks for proof and for a clear link between the claimed reduction and development efficiency. This is not a complaint that the slide is too simple. It is a demand that the strongest claim have the strongest evidence. [2]

Encore also demonstrates that YC's brevity rule is not a universal ten-slide mandate. A 24-slide deck can be defensible when each additional slide advances a complex enterprise or technical narrative. The test is whether the reader is receiving necessary decision information or merely watching the founder repeat a slogan.

### Dropbox and Mixpanel: provenance and stage discipline

A VCMatch page lists a Dropbox 2007 seed deck with 17 slides, $1.2M raised, and a 2007 date. Its page includes an opening description and an outcome claim, while another artifact page did not expose actual slide sections in the available evidence. The conclusion is that Dropbox is a usable secondary case for structure and product explanation, but not a fully verified primary-slide transcription from the pages available here. [39] [35]

Mixpanel's public Slideshare title says it was the pitch deck used to raise $65M. That makes it a useful example of later-stage evidence density and product/metrics presentation, but it is not evidence of seed-stage norms. Mixing stages produces bad advice: a Series B financial slide cannot automatically be required of a pre-revenue seed company. [37]

## 3. DocSend: what the data can and cannot say

DocSend's Startup Index is useful because it measures deck interactions and time spent rather than relying only on founder anecdotes. The metrics page defines pitch-deck interest through investor deck interactions, investor time spent, and founder activity. One available period reports average time on a deck at 2 minutes 30 seconds, while a DocSend design page reports 2 minutes 42 seconds. These are period-specific measurements, not timeless laws. [29] [43]

A secondary report retrieved in this research claims average review time fell to 2 minutes 14 seconds in 2024 from 3 minutes 47 seconds in 2023. Because the retrieved page is not the primary DocSend report, the figure should be used as a reported benchmark with a source-quality caveat, not as an unquestionable universal. [44]

The most strategically important DocSend finding available here concerns unsuccessful decks. In 2023, unsuccessful decks received 110% more investor time on traction slides and 85% more time on business-model slides. This is counterintuitive: more time can mean confusion, attempted verification, or unresolved questions rather than enthusiasm. [16] [16]

That finding changes how founders should interpret engagement analytics. If a reader spends much longer on a business-model slide, do not automatically celebrate. First ask whether the slide is clear enough to understand, whether definitions and units are present, and whether the model is internally consistent. A professional follow-up uses engagement as a diagnostic signal, not as a substitute for judgment.

The available evidence does not justify a universal statement that investors always skim the same slides or that one fixed slide count is optimal. YC provides a more defensible rule: treat slides as the first slide of a set, keep n=1 where possible, and avoid sets larger than three in a seed deck. Secondary pages recommend a 10-14-slide core, while real cases in this report range from 11 to 24 slides. [6] [45] [2]

The practical standard is a short core narrative with a deliberate appendix. The first three slides should establish the company, customer problem, and solution. The following slides should earn the right to add detail by answering the next investment question. If a slide cannot be understood without a live explanation, rewrite it or move the detail to an appendix.

## 4. Executive summaries and one-pagers

YC provides the clearest one-page rule in the evidence: an executive summary should be one or two pages, with one page better. It should include vision, product, team with location and contact information, traction, market size, and minimum financials such as revenue if any and prior and current fundraising. YC also says the slide deck should be a coherent leave-behind and that graphics, charts, and screenshots are more powerful than lots of words. [19]

A one-pager is not simply a shrunken deck. Fundreef defines an investor one-pager as a single-page startup summary intended to earn a meeting and says it condenses value proposition, market opportunity, traction, team, and funding ask into a visual format digestible in 30-90 seconds. The time estimate is guidance from that page, not a DocSend universal. [23]

The strongest one-page architecture is an eight-block sequence: header, vision or company purpose, problem, solution, market, traction, business model, team, and ask/contact. Different templates merge or split these blocks, but the underlying test is stable: a reader should understand what exists, who wants it, why it can be large, why this team can win, what evidence exists, and what the company wants. [33] [20]

The header must do more work than a logo. It should contain the company name, a precise one-line promise, and enough context to tell the reader whether the company belongs in the investor's area of interest. A phrase like “AI platform” is not a sufficient promise because it describes a technology category rather than a customer outcome. YC's jargon and Email Test rules apply directly to this top section. [17]

The problem and solution blocks should be short enough to scan but specific enough to distinguish the company. A problem sentence should identify the affected customer and consequence; the solution sentence should name the product action and concrete benefit. A screenshot can help if it is legible and captioned. A screenshot that requires a spoken tour is decoration, not evidence.

The market block should define the paying customer and show a defensible market method. YC prefers bottom-up sizing because it narrows the customer and avoids claiming that an entire demographic is available. A one-pager cannot contain a full market study, so it should show the calculation logic and link to a more detailed deck or model when needed. [17]

The traction block must be selective. The written format does not have room for every metric, and YC says numbers should be clear and meaningful. Show the one to three measures that prove the central claim, label the period, and state the denominator or unit. If there is no traction, do not manufacture a proxy; explain product status and the next measurable milestone instead. [6]

The team block should name founders, locations, relevant credentials, and contact details. It should show why the founders are suited to this problem, not list every advisor or logo. The ask block should state current fundraising, prior fundraising, use of proceeds, and the milestone the money purchases. YC's minimum-financials guidance makes this written standard explicit. [19] [19]

A one-pager should be designed for scanning but not for visual noise. Use a clear hierarchy, short paragraphs, captions that state conclusions, and enough whitespace for the reader to distinguish sections. The goal is not to make the page look like a poster; the goal is to reduce the cognitive work required to evaluate the company.

## 5. Amateur versus professional: a working review rubric

A professional deck begins with reader comprehension. YC's recommendation to eliminate jargon, acronyms, marketing speak, and ambiguous terms is a direct test. The reviewer should be able to explain the product in different words after reading the first section. If not, the deck is not ready, regardless of polish. [17]

A professional deck also makes the narrative causal. The problem creates the need for the solution; the solution creates the value; the insight explains why this team can deliver it; traction shows that the value is real; the business model explains revenue; the market explains upside; the ask explains acceleration. An amateur deck treats these as independent topics and expects the investor to construct the logic.

The contrast is visible in Encore. Its positioning slides lead into a product challenge, and the reviewer says the value proposition is extremely clear. The deck also repeats the core message in a more visual form. Repetition is professional when it reduces ambiguity; repetition is amateur when it merely increases slide count without adding a new angle. [2] [2]

The contrast is also visible in Buffer. The deck reportedly had a weak problem slide but clear traction and unit economics. That combination can work when the evidence is strong, but it is not a general defense of weak storytelling. A professional reviewer should identify both truths: the deck's proof helped it succeed, and the missing problem explanation remains a weakness that a less proven company could not afford. [26]

Professional design is functional. YC says the seed deck is intentionally simple in design. A simple deck can still use strong typography, visual hierarchy, screenshots, diagrams, and charts, but every visual should answer a question. If the chart needs a paragraph of narration, add context or redesign it. If a graphic has no role in the decision, remove it. [6] [6]

Professional evidence is labeled. Dates, units, denominators, customer definitions, and source notes prevent a reader from confusing a cumulative total with a monthly rate or a signed pilot with revenue. The Encore criticism of “hours to minutes” is a model: the bigger the claim, the more explicit the proof should be. [2]

Professional asks are operational. YC's example connects capital to a one-year destination and its fundraising guide connects capital to months of operation, dilution, and the next fundable milestone. An amateur ask says “we are raising to grow.” A professional ask says “we are raising X to fund Y period of work, reach Z milestone, and make the next financing question easier to answer.” [6] [19]

## 6. Framing the ask, milestones, and use of funds

YC's funding framework begins with a tradeoff. The amount raised determines how much progress can be purchased, affects credibility with investors, and creates dilution. The ask therefore cannot be chosen only by copying a market headline or a peer's round size. It should be the amount that funds a believable plan to the next meaningful milestone. [8] [19]

YC recommends, where possible, raising enough to reach profitability, while recognizing that some companies need a follow-on round. For companies that need another round, the target is usually the next fundable milestone, which the guide describes as typically 12 to 18 months later in relevant cases. Founders should also prepare plans for different raise amounts and explain that the difference changes speed rather than whether the company can succeed. [19]

The guide provides an illustrative operating calculation: an engineer costs about $15K per month all-in; five engineers for 18 months implies $1.35M. This is an example, not a universal seed budget. Its value is the method: show the operating assumptions behind the ask instead of presenting a round amount without a model. [19]

The use-of-funds slide should be a bridge from money to outcomes. Allocate capital to the specific work required for the next milestone, then state the measurable result of that work. “Hiring and marketing” is too vague. “Add two engineers to ship the enterprise permissions layer and fund customer acquisition to reach the stated activation milestone” is decision-ready if the company can support the assumptions.

OpenVC's ask guidance frames the slide around three questions: how much is being raised, how the money will be used, and what investors can expect in return. Its milestone guidance recommends a clean timeline and warns against overcrowding. These are general recommendations rather than empirical laws, but they operationalize YC's ask principle. [41] [40]

Dilution belongs in the underlying plan even when it is not the largest visual on the slide. YC says giving up as little as 10% can be wonderful, that most rounds may require up to 20%, and that founders should try to avoid more than 25%. These figures are YC guidance from its published document, not a recommendation to force every company into one financing structure. [19]

A strong ask slide can therefore use this structure: “We are raising X. This funds Y months of operation. The capital goes to A, B, and C. By the end of the period we expect to reach milestone Z, which establishes the next fundable stage. We have already raised or invested Q. We are seeking the remaining amount on the stated terms.” If a founder cannot complete those sentences without hand-waving, the ask is not ready.

## 7. Recommended production specifications

### Core deck

Use a ten-unit YC-inspired core: title, problem, solution, progress, metrics, insight, business model, market, team, and ask. Merge progress and metrics when the story is early; split them when the chart and explanatory context cannot remain legible together. Add a product slide when the product cannot be understood from the solution slide, but do not repeat the same content. YC explicitly permits slide sets when needed while warning against excessive sets. [6]

Write the slide titles as conclusions, not labels. “We reduce deployment delay from hours to minutes” is stronger than “Value Proposition,” but only if the deck proves the claim or clearly labels it as a target. “Our first users return weekly” is stronger than “Traction,” but only if the denominator, period, and user definition are visible.

Put the most important comprehension burden in the first three slides. The first slide tells the reader what the company does. The second gives the customer pain. The third gives the product response. This follows YC's structure and protects against shrinking review times reported by DocSend-related sources. [6] [29]

### Appendix

Use an appendix for depth that is useful after interest exists: detailed product architecture, full market calculations, cohort charts, customer references, competitive matrices, regulatory detail, financial model, and technical roadmap. This report stays within deck and written-material scope; the appendix is still part of the deck artifact, but it should not carry the core narrative.

### One-pager

Use one page whenever possible. Put the company promise and contact at the top, then arrange the page into eight compact blocks: purpose, problem, solution, market, traction, business model, team, and ask. YC explicitly adds vision, product, team location and contact, traction, market size, and minimum financials. [19]

The one-pager should be usable without a presenter. Every chart needs a title that states its conclusion. Every metric needs a date or period. Every acronym should be expanded or removed. Every section should earn its space by answering a likely investor question.

### Review checklist

1. Can a smart friend explain the company after the two-sentence Email Test? [17]
2. Is the customer specific enough for a bottom-up market calculation? [17]
3. Does progress show output relative to time worked? [17]
4. Is there one specific insight rather than a generic problem statement? [17]
5. Is the business model singular and understandable? [17]
6. Does the team slide show founder fit rather than advisor decoration? [6]
7. Does every chart state what the reader should conclude? [6]
8. Does the ask state amount, period, use, and milestone? [6] [19]
9. Can the one-pager stand alone in one page? [19]
10. Are reconstructed decks and secondary claims labeled by provenance? [22] [15]

## Synthesis

Across the cases, four dimensions explain most of the variation: compression, education, proof, and provenance. Airbnb compresses a new marketplace into a familiar transaction. Coinbase spends scarce slide space educating investors about a new category before presenting its product. Encore uses more slides because the technical and enterprise story requires positioning, product explanation, roadmap, and validation. Buffer demonstrates that strong proof can compensate for weak narrative, while Uber demonstrates that later success does not make every historical slide professional. [9] [46] [2] [26] [24]

The mechanism differs by stage and category. Consumer marketplaces benefit from a visible user exchange; infrastructure companies need a product path and an explanation of technical value; unfamiliar categories need market education; metrics-heavy products need a clear relationship between usage and revenue. The common standard is not a fixed template. It is that each slide removes one material uncertainty without introducing avoidable confusion.

The main tension is brevity versus completeness. YC favors clarity, concision, simple design, and small slide sets, but its own framework allows additional slides where complexity demands them. Real examples range from 11-slide Coinbase and Airbnb reconstructions to Encore's 24-slide deck. DocSend data shows short review windows, but a short deck that omits the model, team, or ask is not efficient; it is incomplete. [6] [6] [2] [29]

The second tension is attention versus comprehension. DocSend reports that unsuccessful decks received substantially more time on traction and business-model slides. That finding means time is ambiguous: it may be interest, confusion, or verification effort. Founders should optimize for fast understanding, not maximum dwell time. [16] [16]

The third tension is simplicity versus proof. Coinbase's bare-bones structure and Airbnb's sparse historical deck show that simplicity can be powerful. Encore shows the limit: a striking claim such as hours to minutes needs evidence. Professional simplicity removes unnecessary information; amateur simplicity removes necessary support. [27] [2]

The fourth tension is template authority versus source provenance. YC's pages provide direct guidance. TechCrunch teardowns provide reviewer analysis. Secondary libraries provide accessible reconstructions, but their claims about original dates, amounts, and slide lists can differ. Stripe, Dropbox, Airbnb, Coinbase, Notion, and Buffer should be cited with the specific artifact or analysis used. Linear and Plaid should remain (UNVERIFIED) in this run. [22] [39] [9]

The decision rule is simple: build a short, stand-alone core deck and a one-page leave-behind that tell the same story at different resolution. Use plain language. Prove the strongest claim. Tie the ask to a period and milestone. Make the first three slides self-explanatory. If the founder cannot state what the money buys, the deck is not finished. If the reader cannot explain the company after the Email Test, the one-pager is not finished. [17] [6] [19]
